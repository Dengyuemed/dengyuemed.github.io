One of the key goals of [precision oncology](https://dengyuerx.com/product-category/prescription-medicines/oncology/) is to identify more targeted treatment strategies based on the molecular characteristics of a tumor. Among the genes involved in tumor development and progression, KRAS has long been a major focus of cancer research and targeted drug development. The KRAS gene encodes a protein involved in regulating cell growth, differentiation, and survival. When specific KRAS mutations occur, related signaling pathways may become abnormally activated, promoting tumor cell proliferation and survival.

Historically, developing drugs that directly target KRAS has been challenging because of the protein's structural characteristics and biological functions. As researchers have gained a deeper understanding of different KRAS mutation subtypes and drug-design technologies have advanced, KRAS-targeted therapy has made significant progress. KRAS G12C inhibitors have provided new treatment options for certain patients, while next-generation inhibitors targeting other KRAS mutation subtypes and active-state RAS proteins are expanding the scope of research in precision oncology.

To understand the progress in this field, it is helpful to first examine the biological role of KRAS and how different mutation subtypes influence treatment strategies.

## 1. Understanding KRAS Mutations: From Genetic Alterations to Abnormal Tumor Signaling

KRAS mutations are important molecular alterations found in several types of solid tumors. Their clinical significance lies not only in the biological characteristics they may indicate but also in their potential to identify therapeutic targets. However, KRAS is not a single, uniform therapeutic target. Different mutation subtypes may vary in their biological behavior, prevalence across tumor types, and sensitivity to specific drugs. Their clinical relevance must therefore be interpreted in the context of the individual molecular profile.

KRAS belongs to the RAS gene family and encodes a small GTPase involved in cellular signal transduction. Under normal conditions, KRAS cycles between active and inactive states to help regulate cell growth, differentiation, and survival. Oncogenic mutations can disrupt this regulatory process, resulting in sustained or abnormal activation of downstream signaling pathways and contributing to tumor development and progression.

KRAS mutations can occur at several positions, with common variants including G12C, G12D, G12V, G12A, G13D, and Q61 mutations. Among these, G12C is an important subtype for which selective targeted drugs have entered clinical practice. G12D has attracted considerable attention in research involving pancreatic cancer and other malignancies, while G12V is also associated with multiple solid tumor types. Because different subtypes have distinct molecular characteristics and biological behaviors, a drug developed for one mutation does not necessarily work effectively against another.

From a precision oncology perspective, identifying the specific KRAS mutation is a key step in assessing potential targeted treatment options. Clinical teams need to consider the tumor type, disease stage, previous treatments, and other molecular characteristics when determining whether a particular therapy may be appropriate. A deeper understanding of KRAS mutation biology also provides the foundation for developing inhibitors with different mechanisms of action.

## 2. The Evolution of KRAS-Targeted Therapy: From G12C Inhibitors to Next-Generation RAS Inhibitors

The development of KRAS-targeted therapy illustrates how cancer drug research has progressed from identifying specific molecular abnormalities to designing drugs that act on those targets. The clinical use of KRAS G12C inhibitors has demonstrated that the KRAS protein, once considered particularly difficult to target, can be addressed through rational drug design. However, because KRAS mutations are diverse, drugs designed for a single subtype cannot address every patient with a KRAS-related tumor. Researchers are therefore continuing to investigate next-generation RAS inhibitors with broader activity profiles and different mechanisms of action.

KRAS G12C inhibitors exploit a specific binding site associated with the G12C mutation to interact with the target protein and inhibit related signaling. These drugs have introduced new treatment options for certain patients with KRAS G12C-mutated cancers and have helped accelerate the development of therapies targeting other KRAS mutation subtypes. Their use, however, is generally limited by the specific mutation, tumor type, previous treatment history, and approved indications.

Beyond G12C, other subtypes, including KRAS G12D and G12V, remain important areas of drug development. Researchers are investigating selective inhibitors designed to recognize these mutations, as well as strategies that interfere with different functional states of RAS proteins. One important approach is to inhibit active-state RAS proteins, thereby disrupting their interactions with downstream effector proteins. Such strategies may broaden the scope of RAS-targeted therapy, although the clinical efficacy and safety of investigational agents must still be established through clinical studies.

Next-generation RAS inhibitors do not eliminate the need to distinguish between mutation subtypes or tumor types. Drugs can differ in their mechanisms of action, clinical trial populations, approved indications, and safety profiles. Understanding these differences helps place advances in KRAS-targeted therapy in the proper context and provides a foundation for evaluating investigational agents such as daraxonrasib.

## 3. Daraxonrasib: Research Progress in Next-Generation RAS-Targeted Therapy

Daraxonrasib, also known by its development code RMC-6236, is an investigational RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor developed by Revolution Medicines. Unlike selective inhibitors that target only the KRAS G12C mutation, daraxonrasib is designed to act on active-state RAS proteins and target multiple common oncogenic RAS variants. Its development reflects efforts to move beyond the limitations of single-subtype inhibition. Its precise clinical applications, however, depend on the available clinical evidence and regulatory approvals.

Mechanistically, daraxonrasib uses a ternary-complex approach. The drug first binds to cyclophilin A (CypA). The resulting complex then interacts with active-state RAS proteins, disrupting their interactions with downstream effector proteins and interfering with signal transmission. This mechanism differs from the specific binding approach used by conventional KRAS G12C inhibitors, making daraxonrasib an important investigational agent in the development of next-generation RAS-targeted therapies.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is a major area of research in RAS-targeted therapy because KRAS mutations are highly prevalent in this cancer type. Daraxonrasib's clinical development has also focused on patients with pancreatic cancer. Its research significance lies not only in exploring a new mechanism of action but also in evaluating whether therapies targeting abnormal RAS signaling can provide meaningful clinical benefits to specific patient populations.

It is important to distinguish between drug development progress, clinical trial results, and formal marketing authorization. Daraxonrasib's regulatory status, indications, and availability may differ across countries and regions. Approval in one market does not mean that the drug has also been approved in other jurisdictions.

Overall, daraxonrasib represents the broader direction of RAS-targeted research, moving from inhibition of a single mutation subtype toward more extensive molecular targeting strategies. Its clinical significance must be assessed in light of formal regulatory information, clinical research data, and individual patient circumstances. This development also provides a broader context for understanding advances in KRAS-targeted drugs in China.

## 5. Clinical Value and Practical Challenges of KRAS Precision Oncology

The value of KRAS-targeted therapy extends beyond advances in drug development. It also reflects the growing role of molecular profiling and individualized assessment in cancer treatment. However, precision oncology does not mean that a treatment decision can be made solely on the basis of a single genetic test result. Whether a patient can receive a particular medicine depends on several factors, including tumor type, disease stage, previous treatments, overall health, drug safety, and the formally approved indications.

Molecular testing is an essential foundation for this treatment approach. Depending on the patient's circumstances, the clinical team may select a validated tissue-based or blood-based testing method to identify the presence and specific type of a mutation. Test results can help assess potential targeted treatment options, but sample quality, testing coverage, and interpretation may all affect the conclusions. Results should therefore be evaluated by qualified healthcare professionals in conjunction with the patient's clinical information.

Drug resistance remains another challenge in targeted therapy. Tumor cells may acquire additional molecular alterations, activate alternative signaling pathways, or develop other adaptive mechanisms that reduce sensitivity to an existing drug. Researchers are therefore investigating not only new inhibitors but also resistance mechanisms, combination treatment strategies, and the appropriate sequencing of therapies. Treatment decisions should be guided by clinical evidence rather than by an isolated mutation result.

In addition to efficacy and safety, drug accessibility and compliant supply are important considerations as medicines move from development into clinical practice.
