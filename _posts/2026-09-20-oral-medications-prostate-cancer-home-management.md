As prostate cancer treatment continues to evolve, some patients no longer need to rely entirely on treatment administered in a hospital or clinic. For eligible patients, certain prostate cancer therapies can be taken orally, allowing them to follow their physician's treatment plan at home while undergoing regular follow-up visits, laboratory tests, and, when necessary, imaging examinations to evaluate treatment response.

This approach can generally be understood as home-based management. However, it is important to clarify that home-based management does not mean that patients should purchase medications on their own, adjust doses independently, or manage their cancer treatment without medical supervision. ## Why Can Some Prostate Cancer Treatments Be Administered at Home?

Prostate cancer is a malignancy that is closely associated with androgen signaling. Androgens, particularly testosterone, can stimulate the growth of certain prostate cancer cells through androgen receptor-related pathways. As a result, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) and medicines that target the androgen receptor pathway have become important components of prostate cancer treatment.

Some hormonal therapies can be administered orally. For eligible patients, this means that part of their daily treatment can be completed at home rather than requiring a visit to a medical facility every time medication needs to be taken.

However, the fact that a medicine can be taken at home does not mean that the entire treatment process can be managed independently at home. Prostate cancer treatment still requires physicians to consider factors such as disease stage, metastatic status, previous treatments, PSA changes, testosterone levels, and the patient's overall health.

A more accurate way to understand home-based management is: Physician develops the treatment plan → Patient takes medication regularly at home → Medical tests are performed as scheduled → Physician evaluates treatment response and safety → Treatment is adjusted when necessary.

## Which Oral Medications Can Be Used in Prostate Cancer Treatment?

Oral treatment for prostate cancer does not involve a single medication or a single treatment approach. Different medicines act on different parts of the androgen pathway, and the appropriate treatment depends on the patient's disease stage and overall treatment plan.

**1. Androgen Receptor Pathway Inhibitors**

Some newer androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) can be administered orally, including:
- Enzalutamide
- Apalutamide
- Darolutamide

These medicines primarily work by inhibiting androgen receptor-related signaling and reducing the stimulatory effects of androgens on prostate cancer cells.

Depending on the disease stage, these medicines may be used in combination with ADT. For some patients with advanced or metastatic prostate cancer, modern treatment strategies may involve ADT combined with another hormonal therapy.

It is important to note that the indications, dosage, administration, and combination treatment strategies differ between medications. The fact that a medicine can be taken orally does not mean that it is appropriate for every patient with prostate cancer.

**2. Abiraterone**

Abiraterone is a medicine that inhibits androgen synthesis and can reduce androgen production in the body.

Unlike some oral medicines that may be used alone within a particular treatment regimen, abiraterone is generally administered in combination with a low-dose corticosteroid. Therefore, patients taking it at home need to follow the prescribed combination treatment carefully rather than focusing only on the abiraterone itself.

Depending on the individual treatment plan, physicians may monitor liver function, blood pressure, potassium levels, and other relevant parameters to evaluate treatment safety.

**3. Oral GnRH Antagonists**

Some patients may also receive an oral GnRH antagonist such as relugolix.

These medicines act on the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis to reduce testosterone levels and produce an androgen-deprivation effect.

Compared with some ADT medicines that require periodic injections, oral administration may provide another treatment option for eligible patients. However, whether this approach is appropriate depends on the patient's disease characteristics, previous treatments, overall health, and the physician's assessment.

## What Does "Home-Based Management" Actually Involve?

![Patient organizing daily medication into a weekly pill organizer at home](/assets/images/home-medication-pill-organizer.png)

Home-based management of prostate cancer is not simply about "taking a pill every day." A structured home-based treatment approach generally includes several key components.

**Taking Medication Regularly**

Patients should take their medication at the prescribed dose and time and maintain a consistent routine whenever possible. For long-term oral treatment, missed doses, accidental duplicate doses, or independently changing the dosage may affect treatment. A pill organizer, smartphone reminder, or medication diary can help patients establish a consistent medication routine.

**Monitoring Changes in Health**

During treatment, patients can record noticeable changes that occur daily or weekly, such as:
- Hot flashes
- Fatigue
- Changes in appetite
- Changes in body weight
- Edema
- Gastrointestinal discomfort
- Changes in sexual function
- Sleep changes
- Other new or unusual symptoms

These records can be shared during follow-up appointments and may help the healthcare team determine whether symptoms could be related to treatment or require further evaluation.

**Regular Laboratory Testing**

Taking medication at home does not eliminate the need for medical monitoring. PSA is an important marker in prostate cancer follow-up. Physicians generally use repeated PSA measurements to monitor trends over time.

Depending on the specific treatment plan, patients may also undergo:
- Testosterone testing
- Liver function tests
- Kidney function tests
- Complete blood counts
- Blood glucose and HbA1c testing
- Lipid testing
- Bone mineral density assessment
- Imaging examinations when necessary

Therefore, patients should view "taking medication at home" and "attending scheduled medical examinations" as two parts of the same treatment process.

**Attending Regular Follow-Up Appointments**

Follow-up is an essential part of home-based management. Physicians need to consider the patient's symptoms, PSA, testosterone, laboratory results, and, when appropriate, imaging findings when evaluating treatment response. Treatment should not be assessed solely on whether the patient feels better or worse.

For patients receiving long-term ADT or other systemic therapies, the appropriate follow-up schedule should be determined by the physician based on disease stage, treatment regimen, treatment response, and the patient's individual circumstances.

## Why Is PSA Important in Home-Based Prostate Cancer Management?

Many patients with prostate cancer ask an important question during treatment: "If my PSA decreases, does that mean the treatment is working?"

PSA is indeed an important marker in prostate cancer follow-up, but a single PSA result should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that treatment has succeeded or failed.

For patients who have been diagnosed with prostate cancer and are receiving treatment, physicians generally monitor PSA over time and pay attention to changes and trends. During hormonal therapy in particular, a reduction in androgen levels can itself lead to a decrease in PSA. Therefore, physicians do not generally determine disease status based on a single PSA result.

A more comprehensive approach is to consider: PSA trends + testosterone levels + symptoms + laboratory findings + imaging when necessary, rather than focusing on a single number.

## What Should Patients Consider During Long-Term Oral Treatment?

Although oral medications can make treatment more convenient, long-term suppression of androgen signaling may be associated with several issues that require attention.

**1. Bone Health**

Long-term ADT may reduce bone mineral density and increase the risk of fractures. For patients receiving long-term ADT, physicians may therefore assess bone density and fracture risk and recommend appropriate measures to support bone health when necessary.

**2. Metabolic Health**

ADT may affect body weight, blood glucose, and lipid levels. Therefore, in addition to monitoring cancer-related indicators, patients receiving long-term treatment may need regular monitoring of:
- Body weight
- Blood pressure
- Blood glucose
- HbA1c
- Blood lipids

Regular physical activity, a balanced diet, and a healthy lifestyle are also important components of long-term disease management.

**3. Fatigue and Quality of Life**

Some patients may experience fatigue, hot flashes, changes in sexual function, or other effects during long-term hormonal treatment. These changes may affect daily activities and overall quality of life. If symptoms persist or significantly interfere with daily life, patients should inform their healthcare providers during follow-up so that the potential causes and appropriate management can be assessed.

**4. Drug Interactions**

Patients with prostate cancer may also take medicines for high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, blood clot prevention, or other conditions. Therefore, before adding any prescription medicine, over-the-counter medicine, dietary supplement, or herbal product, patients should consult a physician or pharmacist.

## How Can Patients Establish a Simple Home-Based Prostate Cancer Management Routine?

Patients receiving long-term oral therapy can establish a simple personal management record.

| Management Area | What Patients Can Do at Home | What the Healthcare Team Does |
|---|---|---|
| Daily medication | Take medication as prescribed and record missed doses | Adjust treatment according to efficacy and safety |
| Symptom monitoring | Record hot flashes, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, and other changes | Assess whether symptoms may be treatment-related |
| PSA | Complete scheduled tests and track trends | Evaluate changes in disease status |
| Testosterone | Complete testing as recommended | Assess the level of androgen suppression |
| Liver and kidney function | Complete scheduled laboratory tests | Evaluate treatment safety |
| Blood glucose/lipids | Monitor regularly and maintain a healthy lifestyle | Manage long-term metabolic risks |
| Bone health | Exercise appropriately and reduce fall risks | Assess bone density and consider bone-protective strategies when appropriate |
| Follow-up | Prepare test results and symptom records before appointments | Evaluate treatment response and determine the next treatment steps |

The purpose of this process is not to make patients responsible for all treatment decisions themselves. Instead, it allows patients to participate in their long-term treatment in a more organized and informed way.

## Oral Treatment Can Make Prostate Cancer Management More Convenient, but It Does Not Mean Self-Treatment

From a patient's perspective, oral anticancer medications may reduce some of the need to travel to a medical facility for treatment and can make long-term therapy easier to integrate into everyday life.

However, the true meaning of home-based management is not that patients manage cancer treatment independently. Rather, it is a long-term treatment model based on regular medication at home and supported by ongoing medical monitoring.

For eligible patients, oral androgen receptor pathway inhibitors, androgen synthesis inhibitors, and certain oral ADT medicines provide different options for long-term prostate cancer treatment. At the same time, continued monitoring of PSA, testosterone, liver and kidney function, metabolic parameters, and bone health remains important.

Patients can therefore think of the overall process as: Choosing an appropriate treatment plan → Taking medication correctly at home → Recording treatment responses → Regular follow-up → Medical evaluation → Treatment adjustment when necessary.
