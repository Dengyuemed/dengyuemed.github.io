#From the Chemotherapy Era to the Precision Medicine Era: What Has Changed in Medical Logic?

Throughout humanity’s long battle against cancer, the medical paradigm has undergone a profound and continuous transformation. From the early chemotherapy era—centered on broad, non-specific cytotoxicity—to today’s precision medicine era, which emphasizes targeted intervention, this shift represents not only technological advancement but also a fundamental reconstruction of underlying medical logic.

Medicine is evolving from experience-driven to data-driven, from disease-centered to patient-centered, and from pursuing average outcomes across populations to identifying the optimal solution for each individual. These changes are reshaping how modern healthcare systems operate.

---

## I. The Chemotherapy Era: A Confrontational Logic Focused on Eliminating Lesions

In the mid-20th century, the introduction of cytotoxic drugs marked the beginning of the chemotherapy era. At that time, medical understanding remained largely at a macroscopic level. Physicians classified diseases based on pathology and applied standardized treatment protocols, making “same disease, same treatment” the dominant approach.

Under this framework, disease was viewed as a target to be eradicated. Chemotherapy adopted a “carpet bombing” strategy—aiming to destroy rapidly dividing cancer cells, but inevitably affecting healthy cells as well. This led to significant side effects such as bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal toxicity, and hair loss.

More importantly, the concept of individual variability was largely unrecognized. Even within the same cancer type, patients could have vastly different molecular drivers. However, due to the lack of diagnostic and analytical tools, these differences could neither be identified nor used to guide treatment.

As a result, clinical outcomes were often inconsistent. Some patients responded well, while others experienced limited benefit or severe toxicity. Treatment evaluation was also relatively narrow—tumor shrinkage was considered the primary indicator of success, often overlooking patients’ overall well-being and quality of life.

---

## II. The Precision Medicine Era: A Reconstructed Logic Centered on Individual Matching

With the advancement of genomics, sequencing technologies, and bioinformatics, medicine has shifted from morphological understanding to molecular-level insight. The emergence of precision medicine marks a fundamental transformation in how cancer is understood and treated.

In this new paradigm, diseases are no longer defined solely by their location or histological appearance but are deconstructed into specific molecular abnormalities. A single type of cancer may consist of multiple subtypes, each requiring a distinct therapeutic approach.

This shift enables a move from uniform treatment strategies to personalized, tailored therapies. Targeted therapies act on specific genetic mutations, while immunotherapies activate the patient’s immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells. Treatment is no longer a one-time decision but an evolving process, guided by continuous monitoring and adaptive adjustments in response to disease progression or resistance.

Equally important is the change in evaluation metrics. Precision medicine goes beyond tumor shrinkage, placing greater emphasis on overall patient benefit, including survival outcomes, quality of life, and treatment burden.

---

## III. From “Drug Effectiveness” to “System Effectiveness”: Redefining Healthcare Capability

As medicine enters the precision era, a critical question emerges:

Even if advanced therapies exist, can all patients truly access them?

The answer is far from straightforward.

The successful implementation of precision medicine depends not only on the drugs themselves but also on an integrated system—encompassing genetic testing, data interpretation, clinical decision-making, and, crucially, drug accessibility and supply stability. In reality, differences in regulatory approval, distribution pathways, and market access across regions often become key barriers to treatment.

Against this backdrop, healthcare competitiveness is shifting from individual drugs to system-level capabilities. Increasingly, organizations with cross-regional integration capacity are playing a vital role in facilitating the global circulation of innovative therapies.

Among them, Dengyue Pharma stands out as a representative example of a professional service provider connecting Chinese innovative drug resources with international markets. By optimizing supply chain integration and cross-border distribution pathways, Dengyue Pharma helps ensure that advanced treatments—including targeted therapies and immunotherapies—can reach healthcare institutions more efficiently and reliably.

This shift highlights a crucial reality:

**Precision medicine is no longer just about whether technologies exist, but whether they can truly reach patients in need.**

---

## IV. Three Key Shifts in Medical Logic

From a broader perspective, the evolution of cancer treatment can be understood as three major transitions:

From “maximizing tumor cell destruction” in the chemotherapy era, to “identifying and targeting specific mechanisms” in the era of targeted and immunotherapies, and finally to “matching the right treatment to the right patient” in the precision medicine era.

Each transition represents a redefinition of efficiency and resource utilization.

Importantly, these approaches are not mutually exclusive. Chemotherapy continues to play a critical role in many treatment settings, complementing newer therapies. The future of oncology is likely to involve combination strategies and increasingly personalized treatment pathways.

---

## V. Conclusion

The transition from “carpet bombing” to “precision targeting” reflects not only scientific progress but also a renewed focus on the human aspect of medicine. The goal of treatment is shifting from merely extending survival to improving both the quality and experience of life.

However, the true impact of this transformation depends not only on innovation in laboratories but also on whether these innovations can cross geographical and systemic barriers to reach real-world patients.

In the future, the defining factor in healthcare may no longer be:

**Who has the most advanced technology, but who can make that technology truly accessible—efficiently and equitably—to those who need it most.**
